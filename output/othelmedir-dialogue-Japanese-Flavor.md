# Othelmedir — A Collection of Lines

*Excerpts from* Dream-Threading *(Yumeguri), Scenes One through Nine.*

> *Translator's note: The Japanese rendered the English source — old-fashioned, ritual-tinged, faintly sinister in its telling — by writing it back as English in the same spirit: the verb arriving last, the subject left unspoken where it could be spared, the layered deference of who-speaks-up-to-whom, the soft tug of the sentence-end. I have kept those textures rather than ironing them flat. Coined terms ("Dream-Threading," "the dream-working art," and the like) are given with their root word beside them where it helps.*

## Scene One

> "Master of the Bedding-house — let it not be asked of me whether I will take the bedstead or the camp-stool. The bedstead, then, it shall be, if there yet remain even one room into which none has already entered. And grant me, so far as it may be done, a chamber set as high as can be had. For upon him who works his craft in the very hour of the stars, the stillness of the upper storeys leans the more tenderly."

## Scene Two

> "Master of the Bedding-house — this figure of 'a thing that nags,' which you yourself have set forth, let me borrow it and say: a thing that nags is a thing that ceases, once the one who frets sinks down into sleep. But the dream-threading (yumeguri), that ceases never. And it is by this very token — more than the faces of the dead that come crowding when the eyelid is shut, more than the visions themselves that are seen — it is this dream-threading that lays your hamlet bare unto its ruin."

> "Othelmedir — such is the name I bear along these roads of mine. I am but a poor pupil-of-the-craft in the dream-working art (musōjutsu / dreamancer), come up of late from the south, and now in service to your person, my lord, this humble one stands. My art, for some years now, has wanted its license. The last who held that writ of leave passed from the world, and left behind no heir to take it up. Yet the knowing of it has not perished along with him. Coin I will not receive. By goods-for-goods and by oaths I do my labor — though such bargaining, you may well let wait until the slate-colored light of the daybreak should strike."

## Scene Three

> "Weirmoth — your black-iron has its uses too, after a fashion. But not all that lies beyond this door may be driven off with so common a weapon."

> "What I cannot use, I do not carry about with me."

> "That which dwells within here — should one leave it loosed without a flinching of hesitation, the folk of the valley-hamlet — before ever the next 'Cleaving Comet' should come blazing brilliantly down — shall be themselves no longer. For the dead, from the inside, with one memory and then another memory, take the living's place. Once, within this tower, two factions (tōtō / cabal) contended one against the other — 'the Red Pew' and 'the Milk-Gathering.' And now, dreaming within the bedding of Sarki Wend, is the elder of the two, and the richer in its yearnings: that is to say, 'the Red Pew.'"

> "Did you not declare it to me — and with no ordinary force, at that — that you yourself have never once dreamed? That within your skull there stands one winter-withered cathedral, its doors barred fast against every visitor who comes?"

> "*Eltesolzo Shai.*" *(In the common tongue, were one to say it — 'With a single sigh, the suture came undone.')*

## Scene Four

> "Step forward, good Weirmoth. Not this room, but the room that lies beyond it — there is where the craft begins. I would have you hold your mouth still, if you would. This air, which for so many years has not passed in and out with breath, and has come up only through the lungs of the dreaming dead — it seems not to be much fond of such a thing as the speech of the living."

> "The next step you owe to your own self, Weirmoth. And after that step, the step that follows upon it. It is in just such a manner that the craft goes forward — without bearing the burden of any further talk than this."

> "One proof. A thing born up out of the quorum (quorum), a thing I have read within the sundry ledgers. Let us tarry here no longer, and go forward."

> "From this side, this will not open, Weirmoth. Not by any spear of yours, and not by any blade that you might think to find within such a hall as this."

## Scene Five

> "This is a treatise, Weirmoth. Which is to say, a thing worthy of close reading, and by no means a thing worthy of the surface-skimming perusal of the idle eye. And this treatise above all — 'Of Medicines and Poisons, by Eleven Compoundings' — I hold to be worthier still of close reading than the most of the books that stand keeping company alongside it upon the shelf."

> "The great cauldron within this tower yet stands stoppered. And yet, its essences seep forth all the same — just as a sealed jar of brine sweats its salt out through the seams. And these essences scatter outward and outward into the air, and it is precisely this that gives rise to the dream-threading. The many deaths the hamlet-folk have dreamed are no phantoms, Weirmoth — they are the leavings, which is to say, the residue."

> "Weirmoth. Here, it seems — here is where your métier (métier) lies."

## Scene Six

> "These crystals buy you nothing of where your concern truly lies, Weirmoth. This decoction that stands before our eyes — this is the article of our compact. What travels within my cloak is only a parting-store laid by against the long road home. Your own share of the coin shall not grow lean on its account, this I will warrant you."

> "'When none is counting, the conjurer's purse grows deep' — indeed, well said. Count, then. When the cauldron has been cleft in two, you may search even unto the very ends of my cloak's lining. But for now, hear me a while. There is an eleventh compounding that the treatise names — 'A Breath from the Silver Dream.' A single breath drawn out from a sleeper who died within sleep itself. The air of this hall holds such a breath. For the death this air first took — the death of a nameless intruder — hangs yet upon the air. To go and fetch it, I myself must enter into the dream that cradles it, and that is a thing I cannot do. But look upon this — this second decoction I have drawn out from the store. This is 'the Witness's Cup.' Should a conjurer well-versed and intimate with the death-art drink this Witness's Cup, every nerve of him is burned, and turned to ash. But one unaccustomed to it will pass through it, and wake."

> "Drink, my lord Weirmoth. You shall come back again — and with no ill aftertaste left lingering upon your lip. And together we shall go down to the cauldron, passing through the wall, bearing the poison I have worked up out of the eleventh compounding."

## Scene Seven

> "Friend Weirmoth. You have slept a heavy sleep. So then, before its savor departs from you, it would be to the profit of us both that you tell over to me the substance of it — what visions came to you within that dream."

## Scene Eight

> "Friend Weirmoth. That road which stood sealed against us shall, within the space of a moment, stand open. And thereafter, we must go down together through the halls that remain — to the cauldron-room, down and down. And so you shall walk before me, and guard this body of mine, whatsoever may lie in wait."

> "There — upon the right hand."

> "Friend Weirmoth. The door that comes next — enter it warily. Once through, keep your body pressed close along the wall as you go. Beyond it lies a ritual ring, graven in chalk (chalk). And upon that chalk, you must by no means set your foot."

> "Comrade Sauerbel. You speak it in error. That lever was the command that was handed down. You gave it, and the command so given, by the very fact of its being given, was obeyed."

> "Before that one rouses the bees, loosen it and strip it away. Before all else, first — its two hands: unmake their working, and return them unto naught."

> "Rudikik, Rudikik, Rudikik." *(Dead tongue — a name read backward.)*

> "Friend Weirmoth. The road has opened below. We must now, at this very hour, go down into it."

> "That is a name I no longer wear upon me."

> "Friend Weirmoth. Keep what ledger you wish, write into it whatever entry pleases you. But there remains to us, yet, within this tower, a matter of business left unfinished."

## Scene Nine

> "Friend Weirmoth. This night you have been given one name out of two dead mouths, and now you stand with steel held up between us two. And it is for just this reason that I will set this whole matter before you, plainly as it is — and set it down but once only. The faction (cabal) of conjurers who built up this cauldron have done no abominable deed whatsoever. The air had been bound away within the jar against the appointed hour of its unsealing. And it was the working of a rival house, and of none other, that broke the both of them — the jar and the hour. The grievous plight that came down upon the hamlet-folk of Sarki Wend followed out of that breaking. It did not arise out of any scheme of mine, nor out of the schemes of my comrades. The silence I kept — I kept it because this land — and likely many another land besides — has never held any patience for the fine weave of such a tale as this. And one who is unjustly blamed is, full often, one who is unjustly hanged. To the cowardice of that silence, I will own myself guilty, friend Weirmoth — and to nothing further beyond it. And the fruit that that cowardice bore, I have, within this single hour, within this single room, already paid back and set right."

> "*Yulledēk Zalleenk. Yulledēk Zalleenk.*" *(Dead tongue — Yulledēk's 'Song of the Closing Curtain.')*

> "It shall come to be as you beg it, my lord Weirmoth."

> "My lord Weirmoth — you strike your bargains in the conjurer's way, then."
